# Christian Theology Curriculum — Read-only content audit

**Status: findings reported; doctrine text not edited.** The curriculum source remains unchanged. In particular, the Module 16 traditions diagram is reproduced as supplied rather than silently repaired.

## Coverage and checks

- Inspected the structured content for all 18 modules and all 220 numbered topics, including explanation, concepts, references, context, and takeaway fields.
- Verified the data structure: 18 modules; 220 topics numbered continuously from 1 to 220; no duplicate or missing topic numbers; all topics have the required fields.
- Reviewed obvious historical, biblical-reference, confessional-framing, and internal-consistency risks. The source is a reconstructed syllabus, not an official TBUGEUSA curriculum.
- This is a **first-pass academic content audit**, not a critical edition: not every biblical citation has been independently checked against a Bible edition or a specialist reference work. Findings below distinguish clear errors from claims needing denominational qualification.

## High-priority factual, citation, or logical corrections

| Location | Finding | Recommended treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **M01-T001** | Calls *theologia* a “Hellenistic coinage” used by Homer. The term is attested in Plato’s *Republic* (379a), around the fourth century BCE; attributing its use to Homer and the “Hellenistic” period needs correction. [2](https://hc.edu/articles/what-is-theology/) | Say the Greek term is attested in Plato, then distinguish its later Christian meaning. |
| **M01-T006** | “*Fides et ratio*” is labelled a Reformation slogan, and the context calls it the “Council of Faith and Reason.” *Fides et Ratio* is Pope John Paul II’s 1998 encyclical letter, not a Reformation slogan or a council. [1](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_14091998_fides-et-ratio.html) | Correct the historical attribution; retain the older Anselm phrase *fides quaerens intellectum* separately. |
| **M02-T013** | Says the “Council of Jamnia” fixed the Hebrew canon around AD 90. There is no good evidence for a formal canon-setting council there; the hypothesis is widely rejected, and the canon’s development should be described more cautiously. [1](https://www.ibri.org/RRs/RR013/13jamnia.html) | Remove the fixed-date/council claim; describe the evidence and uncertainty about the gradual canonization process. |
| **M02-T014–015** | The New Testament canon is said to have acquired its “final, universal shape” through late-fourth-century councils, and both Testaments are said to have reached closed form by the fourth century. This collapses regional lists and later differences among Christian canons into one universal decision. “Syntaxis of Athanasius” also needs a source/title check. | Identify which canon and which regional lists are meant; avoid claiming universal closure by one date. |
| **M02-T016** | Says the Bible was written “in two languages.” Biblical Hebrew, Biblical Aramaic, and Koine Greek are recognized source languages. [2](https://www.learnreligions.com/what-language-was-the-bible-written-in-4158596) | Change “two” to “three” and name them. |
| **M02-T017** | “Pneu-theopneustos” appears to be a malformed Greek term; the word in 2 Timothy 3:16 is *theopneustos*. The statement that inspiration was “reasserted in the Reformation against the Council of Trent’s view” is also misleading: Trent’s decree addresses the canonical books, apostolic traditions, and the Vulgate; it does not deny biblical inspiration. [2](https://history.hanover.edu/courses/excerpts/111ct.html) | Correct the Greek term and describe the actual Reformation dispute as one about authority, canon, tradition, and interpretation—not whether Scripture is inspired. |
| **M02-T022** | “Scripture is revelation committed to memory” appears to be a copy-edit error; the argument describes revelation committed to writing. | Check and correct the intended phrase. |
| **M03-T030 and M04-T047** | Both say God is the “only Author of sin.” That directly contradicts the nearby claim that evil is “not from God,” and it reverses the standard Reformed qualification: God ordains what comes to pass, “yet … neither is God the author of sin.” [3](https://connorpresbyterianchurch.org/wp-content/uploads/westminster-confession-of-faith.pdf) | Treat as a serious likely missing-negation/wording error. Do not leave the sentence as-is in a teaching edition. |
| **M03-T032** | Uses Acts 1:9 for “he cannot lie”; that verse narrates the Ascension. James 1:13 says God is not tempted by evil and does not tempt anyone, but is not the stated “cannot lie” citation. | Replace with a reference that actually supports each clause (for example, Titus 1:2 or Hebrews 6:18 for truthfulness) and separate the claims. |
| **M03-T034** | “The Amen, the faithful and true Witness” is attributed to Revelation 21:5; that title is in Revelation 3:14. “The life that was with the Father” is attributed to John 1:2; the wording is in 1 John 1:2. | Correct both quotations and references. |
| **M04-T043** | After allowing evolutionary accounts, it says “all confessing Christians” affirm the human soul is God’s direct work. That is an overgeneralization and the soul/body account itself is contested among Christians. | Present this as a particular theological position or identify the relevant traditions; do not universalize it. |
| **M05-T059** | “Avedia” is given as the term for iniquity/crookedness. This looks like a transliteration error and needs a Hebrew/Greek lexicon check before publication. | Verify the original-language term and transliteration; do not retain it without a source. |
| **M05-T062** | Says the Arminian claim is that the natural will can “of itself” turn to God. This misstates Arminian/Wesleyan teaching on prevenient grace; it also conflicts with M16-T192, which correctly associates those traditions with prevenient grace. | Rewrite as a comparison of Reformed and Arminian accounts, including prevenient grace, rather than attributing unaided choice to Arminians. |
| **M07-T080** | Says the virgin birth is “affirmed by all four Gospels.” Explicit virgin-conception narratives appear in Matthew and Luke; Mark and John do not narrate one. Galatians 4:4 and 2 Timothy 2:8 do not establish a virgin conception. [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_birth_of_Jesus) | Change “all four” to Matthew and Luke; distinguish the doctrine’s theological rationale from what each cited text says. |
| **M07-T087** | Says “the great councils” affirm penal substitution as the central meaning of the atonement. That overstates what the ecumenical council definitions state; the specific penal-substitution formulation is associated especially with Reformation and later confessional theology. A recent survey notes that atonement ideas were present in council-era debates but not explicitly placed in the doctrinal definitions. [1](https://cf.sbts.edu/equip/uploads/2024/05/SBJT-27.3-Complete.pdf) | Attribute penal substitution to the Reformed tradition and present it alongside other atonement models, rather than attributing it as the councils’ defined centre. |
| **M15-T179** | The Edict of Milan (313) is described as ending persecution and beginning the establishment of the Christian faith in the empire. It granted religious toleration; Nicene Christianity became the official state religion under Theodosius I in 380. [1](https://www.britannica.com/question/When-did-Christianity-become-the-official-religion-of-the-Roman-Empire) | Separate toleration under Milan from later imperial establishment; avoid presenting Constantine’s policy as an instantaneous, empire-wide end to all persecution. |
| **M15-T180** | Groups the Apostles’ Creed with creeds “defined at the councils.” The Apostles’ Creed developed from early baptismal confessions and reached its present form centuries later; it was not defined at an ecumenical council. [1](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Apostles-Creed) | Distinguish the Apostles’ Creed from the Nicene Creed and Chalcedonian Definition. |
| **M15-T183** | “Orthodox period” is described as the Eastern Orthodox Church from the ecumenical councils to the present, alongside a post-Reformation period. This conflates Eastern Orthodoxy with **Protestant/Reformed Orthodoxy**, a separate historiographical term. | Rename and explain the period intended; do not use “Orthodox” ambiguously. |
| **M15-T196** | Places Augustine “in the fifth century” at the beginning of a chapter on medieval theologians. Augustine (354–430) is normally treated as a late-antique/patristic theologian; the topic spans distinct periods. | Separate patristic/late antique theology from medieval scholasticism, or title the span explicitly. |
| **M10-T125** | The postmillennial definition (“the millennium is the present age”) overlaps with common amillennial descriptions and omits the distinctive postmillennial expectation of broad gospel success before Christ’s return. Postmillennial views vary, so this needs a careful distinction rather than a one-sentence binary. [1](https://learn.ligonier.org/articles/millennial-maze) | Define the views by both timing and expected character of the millennium; note variation within each view. |

## Confessional framing and balance

The source repeatedly presents Reformed or evangelical positions in a generic “Christian theology” voice. This is most visible in M01-T026 (sola Scriptura as the final court), M05-T057 and T062 (covenant of works and total depravity), M06-T070–071 (effectual call and monergistic regeneration), M08-T094–108 (Reformed ordo salutis, imputed righteousness, perseverance), and M09-T112–119 (elder government, covenant baptism, Reformed Lord’s Supper, regulative principle, and Reformed marks of the Church). Those are valid positions to teach, but should be labelled as such or compared with Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Arminian/Wesleyan, and other relevant accounts where the topic promises an ecumenical survey.

Other examples needing clear attribution include M12-T153’s conception-to-natural-death bioethical stance, M13-T161’s claim that proclamation is the primary mission method, and M13-T164’s exclusivist statement on salvation. The audit does not endorse or reject those positions; it flags the need to identify the theological lens and represent major Christian alternatives fairly.

### Module 16 diagram — preserve and flag

The **REFORMED** column currently says that tradition, reason, and experience operate “under the magisterium’s guidance,” then describes “Papal primacy.” Those are Roman Catholic claims, not Reformed distinctives. Row 2’s reference to sacraments as means of grace is more nuanced and is not by itself a Catholic marker. The diagram remains unchanged in the rebuilt deck to preserve the supplied content; this is an editorial/doctrinal correction and requires explicit authorization.

## Further academic-quality issues

- **M13-T160 (mission history):** The short history risks treating the Crusades as a missionary period and makes the late-eighteenth-century Protestant movement sound like the start of global Christian mission. It should distinguish military crusading from missionary activity and include early-modern Catholic and global histories; the West-to-world timeline is not the whole history. [4](https://churchforvancouver.ca/christianity-a-missionary-religion-part-i-history-of-missions)
- **M17-T206:** “Higher criticism,” form criticism, and redaction criticism are framed primarily as criticism “of unbelief and doubt” to be answered. These are scholarly methods, not a single anti-faith position; distinguish method from philosophical conclusions.
- **M17-T210–211:** The “philosophy = reason / theology = faith” and “science = how / theology = why” dichotomies are too simple for university-level treatment. Both disciplines use reasoning, and there are areas of overlap and disagreement.
- **M14-T167–175:** These entries are highly repetitive, largely reusing the same “engage X in light of the faith / witness to X” template. They need substantive, topic-specific material and examples before being treated as lecture-ready content.
- **M18-T213–220:** The research module is coherent as an outline but remains generic; add concrete methodology, source-evaluation practice, citation style, ethics procedures, and model research questions for academic use.
